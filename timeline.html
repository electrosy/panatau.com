History

Timeline

Dated stops, not a yearbook. The map changed more than once. Keep the stages separate. Dress across the same span is on wardrobe — imagined plates, not museum cases.

  1. 6th century BCE – Tomis

    Greek settlers from Miletus founded Tomis on the Black Sea coast — today’s Constanța. Grain, fish, and ships made the harbor matter before any later capital inland. Ovid was exiled here under Augustus; the place kept a Greek and then Roman face long after the inland kingdoms rose and fell. See Constanța.

  2. 1st century BCE – early 2nd century CE — Getae and Dacians

    North of the Danube, Geto-Dacian groups held the Carpathian basin and the hills beyond. Burebista briefly pulled a large kingdom together in the first century BCE; Decebalus faced Rome two centuries later from a fortified highland core. Ancient writers often paired them with the Thracians south of the river. They were neighbors and kin in language and custom, not one court. See Thrace and Dacia.

    What people wore. Field and house work sat in a short linen or wool tunic, a belt pouch, and cloth wrapped at the calf. Elite dress on the plate is a longer patterned coat, a dark cloak with a clasp, and a cap — rank in cloth and length. The drawing pairs garments, not kinds of people. All six plates: wardrobe.

  3. 101–106 — Trajan’s wars; province of Dacia

    Rome conquered Dacia under Trajan after two hard campaigns. The new province sat north of the Danube for a relatively short time by imperial standards — roads, camps, and colonists, then pressure from every side. What stayed was not the administration. It was a Latin-based speech that outlived the legions. See The Latin that stayed.

  4. c. 271 — Roman withdrawal

    Under Aurelian the empire pulled the formal province back south of the Danube. Forts emptied; some people moved with the army, others stayed. The end of the province is not the end of Latin speech in the region — only the end of Roman government north of the river. Prefer that distinction to tidy origin myths.

  5. Late antiquity – early Middle Ages

    After the legions, successive peoples moved through the lower Danube and the Carpathian arc: Goths, Huns, Avars, Slavs, and others whose names fill the chronicles more thickly than the villagers’. Written sources for the Romanian-speaking population in these centuries are thin. Slavonic left words and church practice; the Latin grammar held. Prefer that outline to invented detail.

  6. 10th–13th centuries — Vlachs in the sources

    Byzantine, Hungarian, and other writers begin to name Vlachs — Romance speakers — in the Balkans and along the Carpathian rim. The notices are scattered and often hostile or brief. They mark a people already there when the later principalities take shape, not a sudden arrival with a foundation charter. Daily life still sat in yards and seasonal work more than in any court we can name; see the house.

  7. 14th century — Wallachia and Moldavia

    South of the mountains, Wallachia gathers under early Basarab princes; to the east, Moldavia takes shape in the same century. These are frontier principalities between larger empires, not modern nation-states. Tribute, church endowment, and switching protectors were the ordinary tools of survival. See Three lands, not one court.

    What people wore. Village work kept the ia — the embroidered blouse — with a dark apron and a head cloth. The stitch is regional; see crafts. Boyar dress on the plate is a long patterned caftan and a fur hat. Same lands, different cloth and length.

  8. 14th–16th centuries — Transylvania

    Inside the Carpathian arc, Transylvania sat under the Hungarian crown, with Hungarian nobles, Saxon towns, and Romanian-speaking villages sharing an uneven legal map. After Mohács (1526) the region entered the Habsburg and Ottoman contest as a principality with its own estates. Romanian speakers were many on the ground and late to political voice. The three lands stayed separate.

  9. 1457–1504 — Ștefan cel Mare

    Stephen the Great ruled Moldavia for nearly half a century, fought the Ottomans and neighbors, and left a string of churches. The painted exterior walls of northern Moldavia belong to this world of defense and display — a claim on the ridge as much as a prayer. See Why the walls are painted, and a short portrait on men.

  10. 1600 — Mihai Viteazul

    Michael the Brave briefly held Wallachia, Transylvania, and Moldavia under one person. It was a personal union in a crisis of Habsburgs, Ottomans, and local estates — not a lasting national state. The three lands went their ways again after his death in 1601. Later schoolbooks read the year as a sketch of one country; hold both the myth and the short fact. See men.

  11. 18th – early 19th century — Phanariot decades

    In Wallachia and Moldavia, Ottoman-appointed Phanariot princes from the Greek elite of Constantinople ran the courts for much of the eighteenth century and into the nineteenth. Offices were bought, taxes pressed hard, and reform talk grew alongside foreign pressure from Russia and the West. The old tribute arrangement was wearing out; boyars and towns wanted a different kind of prince.

  12. 1821 — Tudor Vladimirescu

    Tudor Vladimirescu raised a popular rising in Wallachia against Phanariot rule and local abuse, in the same year as the Greek struggle against the Ottomans. His movement mixed social anger with politics and ended in his death. It did not create a free state, but it marked that the old court order was cracking in public, not only in petitions.

  13. 1848 — revolutions

    Wallachia, Moldavia, and Transylvania each felt the European year of revolutions in their own key: language, land, and who spoke in public. In Wallachia a short provisional government rose and fell; Ana Ipătescu is one of the few women named in that street story — see women. In Transylvania the Romanian petition for rights sat inside a wider Hungarian and imperial crisis. The maps did not rewrite overnight.

  14. 1859 — Cuza and the United Principalities

    Wallachia and Moldavia elected the same prince, Alexandru Ioan Cuza. That double election is the start of the modern state — one administration assembled from two. Land, schools, and a civil code followed; boyar patience did not. Cuza was forced out in 1866. Elena Cuza’s charity work belongs to the same years — see men and women, and How the country was put together.

  15. 1877–1878, 1881 — independence and kingdom

    War against the Ottomans and the treaties that followed made Romania a recognized independent state. Carol of Hohenzollern, already prince, became king Carol I in 1881. Northern Dobruja, and with it Constanța, came to the country in that settlement. A European dynasty sat on a still-rural kingdom.

    What people wore. A village man in this century still wore an embroidered shirt, a vest, and wrapping at the calf. Urban elite women wore a fitted European gown. The gap is work and city fashion. The shirt’s stitch is the same craft as the ia on crafts.

  16. 1912–1913 — Balkan Wars

    Romania stayed out of the First Balkan War, then entered the Second and gained southern Dobruja at the expense of Bulgaria. A short campaign on the country’s southern edge, before the much larger war that would remake the whole map.

  17. 1918 — Greater Romania

    After the First World War, as the old empires broke, assemblies in Bessarabia, Bukovina, and Transylvania voted union with the kingdom. The enlarged map is what people later called Greater Romania. It included many who were not Romanian speakers. Queen Marie’s wartime and diplomatic work sits in that story — see women and A century, read slowly.

  18. 1918–1939 — interwar

    A large new kingdom tried to govern lands that had never shared one law. Land reform, parties, and a constitution that promised more than local power delivered. Schools and railways spread; minorities and borders stayed contested. The 1930s slid toward authoritarianism under King Carol II, with the Iron Guard as a violent rival on the far right.

    What people wore. Workshop and field clothes stayed a shirt, vest, sash, and a heavy shoe. City elite women wore a long tailored coat, gloves, and a small hat, sometimes with an embroidered band that still points to the ia on crafts.

  19. 1940–1944 — Antonescu and the war

    In 1940 Romania lost territory to the Soviet Union, Hungary, and Bulgaria. Ion Antonescu took power and allied with Germany. The state took part in the murder and deportation of Jews and Roma, especially in Bessarabia, Bukovina, and Transnistria. In August 1944 the country turned against Germany. Keep the alliance, the crimes, and the late switch in the same short frame — none of them cancel the others.

  20. 1944–1947 — end of the kingdom

    Soviet occupation and a coerced political order closed in. King Michael’s late wartime coup had broken with Germany; by the end of 1947 he was forced to abdicate and a communist republic stood in the kingdom’s place. The crown’s century was over.

  21. 1947–1989 — communist period

    Arrests, forced collectivization of the land, and later heavy industry under Gheorghiu-Dej and then Nicolae Ceaușescu. The Securitate watched stairwells and workplaces; the regime claimed a national road and built a personality cult on shortage. The 1980s meant rationed heat and light, food exported to pay debt, and queues that taught a generation to wait. Village yards emptied toward the block — the older house did not vanish, but the state’s plan tried to outrank it.

    What people wore. Rural and factory weeks meant a plain dress or a wool jacket — clothes for the shift and the queue. City and office dress on the plate is a dark suit. The woven scarf is household cloth; the stitch is still the one named on crafts.

  22. December 1989 — Revolution

    The rising began in Timișoara and reached Bucharest within days. Ceaușescu and his wife fled, were caught, tried in haste, and shot. Crowds in the square and confusion in the streets — what followed was a rough exit, not a clean door into another kind of country.

  23. 1991 — constitution; the 1990s

    A new constitution framed a republic after the old party state. Parties and property claims returned; privatization and street politics were noisy and incomplete. Emigration began in earnest. The decade was less a triumph than a long unpacking of what the previous forty years had locked down.

  24. 2004–2007 — NATO and the EU

    Romania joined NATO in 2004 and the European Union in 2007. Those dates close a long outbound path from the wartime and communist maps. Membership did not end politics or departure for work abroad; it changed the frame in which both happen.

  25. Present

    Ordinary EU membership, emigration and return, and arguments that still run through language, region, and memory. The same ridges that outlasted every map on this page are still there. Dated stops end; the ground does not.

    What people wore. Everyday dress is a jacket, jeans, and boots. Festival and family days still bring out the ia — the embroidered blouse on crafts — worn with modern trousers. The mix is ordinary. All six plates: wardrobe.

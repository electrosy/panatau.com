History

Timeline

Dated stops, not a yearbook. The map changed more than once. Keep the stages separate.

  1. 6th century BCE – Tomis

    Greek settlers from Miletus founded Tomis on the Black Sea coast — today’s Constanța. The harbor, not a later capital inland, is why the place matters first. See Constanța.

  2. 1st century BCE – early 2nd century CE — Getae and Dacians

    North of the Danube, Geto-Dacian groups held the Carpathian basin. Burebista briefly pulled a large kingdom together; Decebalus faced Rome later. Ancient writers often paired them with the Thracians south of the river. They were neighbors, not one court. See Thrace and Dacia.

  3. 101–106 — Trajan’s wars; province of Dacia

    Rome conquered Dacia under Trajan. The province lasted a relatively short time by imperial standards. What stayed was not the administration. It was a Latin-based speech that outlived the legions. See The Latin that stayed.

  4. Late antiquity – early Middle Ages

    After the legions withdrew, successive peoples moved through the lower Danube and the Carpathian arc. Written sources for the Romanian-speaking population in these centuries are thin. Slavonic left words and church practice; the Latin grammar held. Prefer that outline to invented detail.

  5. 14th–15th centuries — three lands

    Wallachia south of the mountains, Moldavia to the east, Transylvania inside the arc — usually under a Hungarian crown, later Habsburg. No single Romanian state. Princes paid tribute, switched protectors, and built churches to stay visible. See Three lands, not one court.

  6. 1457–1504 — Ștefan cel Mare

    Stephen the Great ruled Moldavia for nearly half a century, fought the Ottomans and neighbors, and left a string of churches. The painted exterior walls of northern Moldavia belong to this world of defense and display. See Why the walls are painted.

  7. 1600 — Mihai Viteazul

    Michael the Brave briefly held Wallachia, Transylvania, and Moldavia under one person. It was a personal union in a crisis, not a lasting national state. The three lands went their ways again after his death.

  8. 18th – early 19th century — Phanariot decades

    In Wallachia and Moldavia, Ottoman-appointed Phanariot princes from the Greek elite of Constantinople ran the courts for much of the eighteenth century. Reform talk and foreign pressure grew; the old tribute arrangement was wearing out.

  9. 1859 — Cuza and the United Principalities

    Wallachia and Moldavia elected the same prince, Alexandru Ioan Cuza. That double election is the start of the modern state — one administration assembled from two. See How the country was put together.

  10. 1877–1878, 1881 — independence and kingdom

    War against the Ottomans and the treaties that followed made Romania a recognized independent state. Carol of Hohenzollern became king in 1881. Northern Dobruja, and with it Constanța, came to the country in that settlement.

  11. 1918 — Greater Romania

    After the First World War, as the old empires broke, assemblies in Bessarabia, Bukovina, and Transylvania voted union with the kingdom. The enlarged map is what people later called Greater Romania. It included many who were not Romanian speakers. See A century, read slowly.

  12. 1918–1939 — interwar

    A large new kingdom tried to govern lands that had never shared one law. Land reform, parties, and a constitution that promised more than local power delivered. The 1930s slid toward authoritarianism under King Carol II, with the Iron Guard as a violent rival on the far right.

  13. 1940–1947 — war and the end of the kingdom

    In 1940 Romania lost territory to the Soviet Union, Hungary, and Bulgaria. Ion Antonescu allied with Germany; the state took part in the murder and deportation of Jews and Roma. In August 1944 the country turned against Germany. By the end of 1947 the king was gone and a communist republic stood in the kingdom’s place.

  14. 1947–1989 — communist period

    Arrests, collectivization, and later industry under Gheorghiu-Dej and then Nicolae Ceaușescu. The regime claimed a national road and built a personality cult on shortage. The 1980s meant rationed heat and light, food exported to pay debt, and a secret police in the stairwell.

  15. December 1989 — Revolution

    The rising began in Timișoara and reached Bucharest. Ceaușescu and his wife were tried in haste and shot. What followed was a rough exit, not a clean door into another kind of country.

  16. 1990s–present

    Parties and property claims returned. Romania joined NATO in 2004 and the European Union in 2007. The present day is ordinary EU membership, emigration and return, and the same ridges that outlasted every map on this page.

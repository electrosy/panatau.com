History

Timeline

Dated stops, not a yearbook. The map changed more than once. Keep the stages separate. Dress across the same span is on wardrobe — imagined plates, not museum cases.

  1. 6th century BCE – Tomis

    Greek settlers from Miletus founded Tomis on the Black Sea coast — today’s Constanța. Grain, fish, and ships made the harbor matter before any later capital inland. Ovid was exiled here under Augustus; the place kept a Greek and then Roman face long after the inland kingdoms rose and fell. The salt edge is still the short Romanian shore on the sea. See Constanța.

  2. 1st century BCE – early 2nd century CE — Getae and Dacians

    North of the Danube, Geto-Dacian groups held the Carpathian basin and the hills beyond. Burebista briefly pulled a large kingdom together in the first century BCE; Decebalus faced Rome two centuries later from a fortified highland core. Those same slopes are still where people meet bear and wolf — see wildlife. Ancient writers often paired them with the Thracians south of the river. They were neighbors and kin in language and custom, not one court. See Thrace and Dacia.

    What people wore. Field and house work sat in a short linen or wool tunic, a belt pouch, and cloth wrapped at the calf. Elite dress on the plate is a longer patterned coat, a dark cloak with a clasp, and a cap — rank in cloth and length. The drawing pairs garments, not kinds of people. All six plates: wardrobe.

  3. 101–106 — Trajan’s wars; province of Dacia

    Rome conquered Dacia under Trajan after two hard campaigns. The new province sat north of the Danube for a relatively short time by imperial standards — roads, camps, and colonists, then pressure from every side. Gold and salt moved on those roads; the forts did not last. What stayed was not the administration. It was a Latin-based speech that outlived the legions. See The Latin that stayed.

  4. c. 271 — Roman withdrawal

    Under Aurelian the empire pulled the formal province back south of the Danube. Forts emptied; some people moved with the army, others stayed. The end of the province is not the end of Latin speech in the region — only the end of Roman government north of the river. The river stayed a border in Roman minds long after the province was gone. Prefer that distinction to tidy origin myths.

  5. Late antiquity – early Middle Ages

    After the legions, successive peoples moved through the lower Danube and the Carpathian arc: Goths, Huns, Avars, Slavs, and others whose names fill the chronicles more thickly than the villagers’. Written sources for the Romanian-speaking population in these centuries are thin. Slavonic left words and church practice; the Latin grammar held. The church that remained was the Eastern one — see Orthodoxy. Prefer that outline to invented detail.

  6. 10th–13th centuries — Vlachs in the sources

    Byzantine, Hungarian, and other writers begin to name Vlachs — Romance speakers — in the Balkans and along the Carpathian rim. The notices are scattered and often hostile or brief. They mark a people already there when the later principalities take shape, not a sudden arrival with a foundation charter. Daily life still sat in yards and seasonal work more than in any court we can name; see the house. Flocks still climb and come down on that older calendar — see shepherds.

  7. 14th century — Wallachia and Moldavia

    South of the mountains, Wallachia gathers under early Basarab princes; to the east, Moldavia takes shape in the same century. These are frontier principalities between larger empires, not modern nation-states. Tribute, church endowment, and switching protectors were the ordinary tools of survival. Monasteries kept the books when the state was small; that habit is on Orthodoxy. The three lands stayed separate for a long time — the line is on borders. See Three lands, not one court.

    What people wore. Village work kept the ia — the embroidered blouse — with a dark apron and a head cloth. The stitch is regional; see crafts. Boyar dress on the plate is a long patterned caftan and a fur hat. Same lands, different cloth and length.

  8. 14th–16th centuries — Transylvania

    Inside the Carpathian arc, Transylvania sat under the Hungarian crown, with Hungarian nobles, Saxon towns, and Romanian-speaking villages sharing an uneven legal map. After Mohács (1526) the region entered the Habsburg and Ottoman contest as a principality with its own estates. Romanian speakers were many on the ground and late to political voice. A Transylvanian sat could sit under a different law than the Hungarian town or the Saxon street at the end of the same valley — see villages. The three lands stayed separate.

  9. 1457–1504 — Ștefan cel Mare

    Stephen the Great ruled Moldavia for nearly half a century, fought the Ottomans and neighbors, and left a string of churches. The painted exterior walls of northern Moldavia belong to this world of defense and display — a claim on the ridge as much as a prayer. See Why the walls are painted, the ridges on north, and a short portrait on men.

  10. 1600 — Mihai Viteazul

    Michael the Brave briefly held Wallachia, Transylvania, and Moldavia under one person. It was a personal union in a crisis of Habsburgs, Ottomans, and local estates — not a lasting national state. The three lands went their ways again after his death in 1601. Later schoolbooks read the year as a sketch of one country; hold both the myth and the short fact. See men and the later line on borders.

  11. Late 17th – 18th century — Transylvania under the Habsburgs

    After the Ottoman retreat, Transylvania passed under the Habsburg court. The diet still privileged Hungarians, Saxons, and Szeklers. A union with Rome was offered to some Romanian communities at the end of the seventeenth century; many stayed Orthodox — that split is on Orthodoxy. In 1784 a peasant rising under Horea, Cloșca, and Crișan burned through the Apuseni. The villages did not gain a political nation. They gained a longer argument with Vienna and with the estates above them. Bukovina passed to the same court in 1775 — an earlier cut, remembered when 1918 looks like a sudden widening. See borders.

  12. 18th – early 19th century — Phanariot decades

    In Wallachia and Moldavia, Ottoman-appointed Phanariot princes from the Greek elite of Constantinople ran the courts for much of the eighteenth century and into the nineteenth. Offices were bought, taxes pressed hard, and reform talk grew alongside foreign pressure from Russia and the West. Greek remained a court language for a time; Romanian print and school talk were already pushing back from below. The old tribute arrangement was wearing out; boyars and towns wanted a different kind of prince.

  13. 1821 — Tudor Vladimirescu

    Tudor Vladimirescu raised a popular rising in Wallachia against Phanariot rule and local abuse, in the same year as the Greek struggle against the Ottomans. His movement mixed social anger with politics and ended in his death. It did not create a free state, but it marked that the old court order was cracking in public, not only in petitions. The Phanariot line in Wallachia ended soon after; Ottoman suzerainty did not.

  14. 1848 — revolutions

    Wallachia, Moldavia, and Transylvania each felt the European year of revolutions in their own key: language, land, and who spoke in public. In Wallachia a short provisional government rose and fell; Ana Ipătescu is one of the few women named in that street story — see women. In Transylvania the Romanian petition for rights sat inside a wider Hungarian and imperial crisis. At Blaj that spring a large gathering asked for language and land in public, and still had to live under the old estates when the year closed. The maps did not rewrite overnight.

  15. 1859 — Cuza and the United Principalities

    Wallachia and Moldavia elected the same prince, Alexandru Ioan Cuza. That double election is the start of the modern state — one administration assembled from two. Land, schools, and a civil code followed; boyar patience did not. Cuza was forced out in 1866. A foreign prince was brought in to make the new state look permanent to the courts of Europe. Transylvania was not in the bargain. Elena Cuza’s charity work belongs to the same years — see men and women, How the country was put together, and the stages on borders.

  16. 1877–1878 — the independence war

    Romania entered the war against the Ottomans beside Russia and declared independence in May 1877. Romanian troops fought hard at Plevna and later at Grivița and Smârdan — a short, costly campaign, not a parade. The treaties that followed, San Stefano and then Berlin, made the state recognized. Northern Dobruja, and with it Constanța, came in that settlement — a sea window the principalities had not held. See the sea, Constanța, and the line on borders.

  17. 1881–1914 — Carol I and the kingdom

    Carol of Hohenzollern, already prince since 1866, became king Carol I in 1881. He reigned a long time: railways, a constitution, a still-rural country, and a court that wanted to look European. The villages kept their own year — harvest, winter, the ia. A European dynasty sat on a kingdom whose map still stopped short of Transylvania.

    What people wore. A village man in this century still wore an embroidered shirt, a vest, and wrapping at the calf. Urban elite women wore a fitted European gown. The gap is work and city fashion. The shirt’s stitch is the same craft as the ia on crafts.

  18. 1912–1913 — Balkan Wars

    Romania stayed out of the First Balkan War, then entered the Second and gained southern Dobruja — the Cadrilater — at the expense of Bulgaria. A short campaign on the country’s southern edge, before the much larger war that would remake the whole map. The new strip was contested from the start; it would go back to Bulgaria in 1940. See borders.

  19. 1914–1918 — Ferdinand, Marie, and the war

    Ferdinand succeeded Carol I in 1914. Romania entered the First World War late, on the Allied side, was occupied, and then found itself among the winners as Austria-Hungary came apart. Queen Marie worked in hospitals and spoke for the Allied cause. Villages emptied toward the front — Ecaterina Teodoroiu is one name from that emptying. Both sit on women. The map that followed is the next stop.

  20. 1918 — Greater Romania

    After the First World War, as the old empires broke, assemblies in Bessarabia, Bukovina, and Transylvania voted union with the kingdom. The enlarged map is what people later called Greater Romania. It included many who were not Romanian speakers. Bucovina’s painted ridges are on north; the line that widened, then later shrank, is on borders. Queen Marie’s wartime and diplomatic work sits in that story — see women and A century, read slowly.

  21. 1918–1939 — interwar

    A large new kingdom tried to govern lands that had never shared one law. Land reform, parties, and a constitution that promised more than local power delivered. Schools and railways spread; minorities and borders stayed contested — see borders. The 1930s slid toward authoritarianism under King Carol II, with the Iron Guard as a violent rival on the far right. Bucharest was the capital; Cluj, Chișinău, Cernăuți, and the Saxon towns had other memories.

    What people wore. Workshop and field clothes stayed a shirt, vest, sash, and a heavy shoe. City elite women wore a long tailored coat, gloves, and a small hat, sometimes with an embroidered band that still points to the ia on crafts.

  22. 1940–1944 — Antonescu and the war

    In 1940 Romania lost territory to the Soviet Union, Hungary, and Bulgaria — the cuts are on borders. Those losses broke the Carol II regime. Ion Antonescu took power with the Iron Guard and then against it, and allied with Germany. Romanian armies went into the Soviet Union. The state took part in the murder and deportation of Jews and Roma, especially in Bessarabia, Bukovina, and Transnistria. In August 1944, with Soviet armies already on the soil, the country turned against Germany. Keep the alliance, the crimes, and the late switch in the same short frame — none of them cancel the others.

  23. 1944–1947 — end of the kingdom

    King Michael’s coup of 23 August 1944 had broken with Germany; Soviet occupation and a coerced political order closed in anyway. Petru Groza’s government in 1945, and elections the next year that were not free, did the rest. By the end of 1947 the king was forced to abdicate and a communist republic stood in the kingdom’s place. The peace left a smaller country than 1918 — that outer line is on borders. The crown’s century was over.

  24. 1948–1965 — party state and collectivization

    Arrests, forced collectivization of the land, and the Canal. Gheorghiu-Dej ran a party state that watched stairwells and workplaces. Grain and yards were pulled into the plan; the older harvest calendar did not vanish, but the state’s claim tried to outrank it. Industry was the promise. Fear was the method. The road still filled on a feast day; much of the week it answered to the collective — see villages.

  25. 1965–1989 — Ceaușescu

    Nicolae Ceaușescu took the party in 1965. A brief opening — the 1968 refusal to join the Prague invasion — then the July Theses, a personality cult, and shortage dressed as a national road. The Securitate watched the stairwell. The 1980s meant rationed heat and light, food exported to pay debt, and queues that taught a generation to wait. Village yards emptied toward the block — the older house did not vanish, but the state’s plan tried to outrank it. Winter in those years was a dark stair and a cold flat as much as snow in the yard — see winter.

    What people wore. Rural and factory weeks meant a plain dress or a wool jacket — clothes for the shift and the queue. City and office dress on the plate is a dark suit. The woven scarf is household cloth; the stitch is still the one named on crafts.

  26. December 1989 — Revolution

    The rising began in Timișoara and reached Bucharest within days. Ceaușescu and his wife fled, were caught, tried in haste, and shot. Crowds in the square, shooting whose chain of command is still argued, and a National Salvation Front that filled the empty chairs. What followed was a rough exit, not a clean door into another kind of country.

  27. 1991 — constitution; the 1990s

    A new constitution framed a republic after the old party state. Parties and property claims returned; privatization and street politics were noisy and incomplete. The early years had miners in Bucharest and a first decade that felt like unpacking, not arrival. A peaceful change of government in 1996 showed the chairs could move without a street war. Emigration began in earnest — locked village gates, money sent home, the ordinary fact on villages. Applications toward NATO and the EU started here, before the accession dates. The decade was less a triumph than a long unpacking of what the previous forty years had locked down.

  28. 2004–2007 — NATO and the EU

    Romania joined NATO in 2004 and the European Union in 2007, in the same intake as Bulgaria, after years of negotiation against the union’s entry rules. Those dates close a long outbound path from the wartime and communist maps. Membership did not end politics or departure for work abroad; once the labor market opened, more people left, and more sent money back. It changed the frame in which both happen. The surveyor’s line on borders did not jump.

  29. Present

    Ordinary EU membership, emigration and return, and arguments that still run through language, region, and memory. The same ridges that outlasted every map on this page are still there — see borders. Dated stops end; the ground does not.

    What people wore. Everyday dress is a jacket, jeans, and boots. Festival and family days still bring out the ia — the embroidered blouse on crafts — worn with modern trousers. The mix is ordinary. All six plates: wardrobe.

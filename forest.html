The forest

Pădure, and the definite pădurea. The woods people mean when they say they went into the forest — not a park with a ticket booth, and not the trees along a town street. The shade past the last house, the path that still has a name.

Where the sat ends

The village does not stop with a sign. It thins. Gardens give way to a strip of grass, then a cart path, then trunks. The last fence is still the yard on the house. The road behind you is still villages. Ahead the light drops, and the dogs already know the line even if the map does not. People walk that edge for wood, for a basket, for a cow that did not come home, for a shortcut to the next sat. A troiță may stand at the fork before the timber starts. Children are told not to go far and then they go as far as the first clearing. This is not wilderness in the brochure sense. It is the same commune, only darker, and the work has a different noise.

October in the beech

Toamna on this slope is not the maize on the porch. That hurry is on harvest. Up here the color is the trees. In the Carpathians the wood people walk in October is often beech, fag — a dull gold, then brown, and the path fills with leaves that were still on the branch last week. Lower, where the hills open toward the village, oak still stands. People say stejar for the one on warmer ground, and gorun where it already mixes with the beech. Oak holds its brown longer. Beech lets go. Higher up the spruce, molid, stays dark and does not join the color. The dates the year still names for this turn are on autumn on the year.

A clear week and the ridge shows through. A wet week and the path is black leaves and mud, and the gold is only overhead. You do not need a map of elevations. You need the habit of noticing which trunks went gold, which are still brown, and which never changed. It is the same commune. The light has gone copper, and the work has a different sound — a cart, or nothing but your own steps.

What you look for

After rain people go for mushrooms, ciuperci, and they still argue over the names the way they argue over a recipe. Wild strawberries, fragi, at the sunny edge. Raspberries, zmeură, in a clearing the light found. Shade in July when the maize is loud. The sound under the canopy: a jay, a creek that does not yet have a map name, your own steps on last year’s leaves. A basket, a stick, a shirt that will smell of resin until evening. You go in and you come back before dark because that is how the village still talks about it. You do not need a trail marker. You need the habit of knowing which path is the one home.

The basket

After rain the basket still goes out. Ciuperci is the general word. The ones people name in October are more particular. Hribi, porcini, in the beech and under the spruce. Gălbiori, chanterelles, the yellow ones, where the moss kept the damp. They are not a promise. A dry month and the woods stay empty. A wet one and the same path has more than last week. Afine, bilberries, are the berry of the higher ground, and that picking is mostly summer. By October the bush is leaves. People still say the word when they mean what the mountain gave, the way they name a month that already passed.

A doubtful cap stays where it grew, or it goes to someone who already knows the names. A picture on a phone is not that person. The pretty one and the one you leave can share a color, and cooking does not settle it. You do not need a field guide on this page. You need the habit of eating only what someone you trust will swear to, and of coming back before dark, because that is still how the village talks about the path.

Wood that becomes something

Timber leaves the woods as a job, not as scenery. Oak for a poartă — that monument is on gates. Fir and oak for a church roof, șindrilă that sheds snow — wooden churches. Beams, a fence post, the winter pile stacked against the wall so January has an argument it can lose — that life is on winter. A man with a cart, later a tractor, later a neighbor who still knows which slope is theirs to cut. The craft of turning a log into a thing that stands is named on crafts. You do not need a catalog of species. You need the habit of asking what the tree became, and whose yard it stands in now.

The pădurar and the pile

A pădurar is not every neighbor with a saw. It is the forester the forest road already knows, the one who can say which slope is marked this year and which is not. When the yard pile will not last, people still ask at the ocol silvic, the forest district, for lemne de foc. Some years the wood comes from there. Some years a household still cuts what is already theirs. You do not need the paperwork. You need the habit of asking whose mark is on the tree, and whose cart it left on.

The winter pile is the part the house can see. Lemne go into a stivă against the wall, or under the eaves, before the snow sits on the slope. The pile’s other life — January, the stove — is on winter. A tractor now, a cart if the lane still takes one. Beech and oak burn long. Spruce lights easier and is gone sooner, and someone older still says which. The beam that will be a gate is a different job, already on gates. This pile is only heat.

Neighbors, not a safari

The bear, the urs, and the wolf, the lup, live in the same woods as the firewood. Deer, cerb, and the smaller căprioară stand in a clearing and then they are gone. The wild boar, the mistreț, uses the same mud as the cart. They are the animals people still meet — that page is wildlife — not a weekend of photographs. Shepherds count the flock against them; that work is on shepherds. In the north the forest is the first wall of the valley — North. A river often starts as a noise between the trees before anyone bothers to name it on a map — rivers. You keep the dogs close. You talk about tracks the way you talk about weather. You do not go looking for a bear the way a brochure looks for a view.

Between the yard and the ridge

This page is only that place: the path past the last house, October in the beech, the basket someone will swear to, the pădurar and the pile, the timber that will be a roof, the rumor of a bear, and the ridge you can see when the trees open.

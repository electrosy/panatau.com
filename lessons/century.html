History

A century, read slowly

The twentieth century here does not have one portrait. Read it as a sequence, and ask who is speaking.

A kingdom that had just become large spent the 1920s trying to govern lands that had never shared a law. Bucharest was the capital. Cluj, Chișinău, Cernăuți, and the Saxon towns had other memories. Land reform broke up some great estates and did not end village poverty. A constitution promised rights that parties, police, and local bosses applied unevenly. Jewish citizens faced exclusion that grew sharper as the European crisis deepened. None of this is a footnote to “what happened next.” It is the ground.

In the 1930s the kingdom slid toward authoritarianism. King Carol II suspended ordinary politics. The Iron Guard, a fascist movement with a mystical and violent style, murdered opponents and was in turn cut down by the palace. When the Second World War reached this map, the state did not choose cleanly or once. In 1940, under pressure from Germany and the Soviet Union, Romania lost northern Transylvania to Hungary, southern Dobruja to Bulgaria, and Bessarabia and northern Bukovina to the Soviet Union. Those losses broke the Carol regime. General Ion Antonescu took power with the Guard and then against it, and allied the country with Germany. Romanian armies went into the Soviet Union. The Romanian state also carried out and enabled the murder and deportation of Jews and Roma, especially in Bukovina, Bessarabia, and Transnistria. That is part of the century. A later alliance with the Allies does not erase it.

In August 1944, with Soviet armies on the soil, King Michael and a group of officers and politicians arrested Antonescu and turned the country against Germany. Romania finished the war on the other side, and still lost Bessarabia and northern Bukovina for good. The peace left a bruised, occupied kingdom. Communists, few in 1944, used the Soviet presence, the ministry of the interior, and a series of elections that were not free. By the end of 1947 the king was gone. The republic that followed was a party state.

The communist decades are not one color either. The early years were arrests, collectivization, and the Canal. Later, under Gheorghe Gheorghiu-Dej and then Nicolae Ceaușescu, the regime claimed a national road, quarreled with Moscow, and built industry and a personality cult on shortage. The 1980s were darkness in the literal sense: heat and light rationed, food exported to pay debt, a secret police in the stairwell. People remember the jokes, the queues, and the small permissions as much as the speeches. A foreign visitor who saw only the parade avenue did not see the apartment at night.

In December 1989 the regime fell in a rising that began in Timișoara and reached Bucharest, with shooting whose full chain of command is still argued. Ceaușescu and his wife were tried in haste and shot. What followed was not a clean door into another kind of country. It was a rough exit from one. Parties returned. Property claims returned. Some of the people who had run things found new chairs. The lesson of this page is not a moral that the century “ended well.” It is a way of reading. Ask who lost land, who was deported, who got a flat, who left. The pictures on this site are not evidence of any of that. They are a place to stand while the harder record does its work.

History

Three lands, not one court

Wallachia, Moldavia, and Transylvania were neighbors for centuries before they were one state.

If you look at a map of Romania today and then try to read a chronicle from the fifteenth century, the names will not line up. There was no single court ruling from the mountains to the sea. There were three political worlds, touching, trading, and fighting, and often paying tribute to a larger power that did not speak their language.

Wallachia lay south of the Carpathians, between the mountains and the Danube. Its princes ruled from camps and then from towns, Târgoviște and later Bucharest among them. The land is a plain that rises into foothills. It looked south and east, toward the river and, for a long time, toward the Ottoman Empire. Tribute was not a metaphor. It was a payment that bought a kind of room to rule at home, and it could be raised, refused, or used as a reason for war.

Moldavia lay to the east of the mountains, running toward the Prut and the black-earth country beyond. Its princes are the ones later schoolbooks lean on hardest, Stephen the Great among them, because the century of their resistance left monasteries, battle names, and a story a new nation could use. The land itself is not a footnote to those princes. It is river valleys, painted churches in the north, and a frontier that spent centuries between Poland, Hungary, the Ottomans, and later Russia. A prince who won a battle still had to feed a court and keep a boyar class that owned the villages.

Transylvania sat inside the mountain arc. For most of this long middle period it was not a Romanian principality in the same sense. It was a voivodeship and then a principality under the Hungarian crown, and later under the Habsburgs. Political rights in the diet belonged to Hungarians, Saxons, and Szeklers. Romanians were the numerous people of the hills and many of the villages, and for a long time they were outside that privileged order. That fact is why Transylvania’s later union with the other two lands was both obvious to Romanian speakers and contested by everyone who had held the old privileges.

None of this was a waiting room for 1859. People lived complete lives inside these arrangements. They paid dues, married, built wooden churches, and sold salt, cattle, and wine along roads that ignored the later national line. The painted monasteries of Bucovina and the wooden gates of Maramureș are not illustrations of a future flag. They are what communities built when the state above them was distant, foreign, or both.

When the modern story says “the Romanian lands,” it is compressing three different relationships to empire into one phrase. Useful, if you remember it is a phrase. Wallachia and Moldavia were principalities whose princes were often confirmed by the sultan. Transylvania was a province of a Christian kingdom and then of a Catholic and later Habsburg order. The Romanian language ran through all three. The law, the tax, and the official faith did not. Hold that split in mind and the nineteenth-century union stops looking like destiny and starts looking like a political achievement, argued, fought over, and unfinished at the edges for a long time after.

Dialects

How Romanian actually varies, without pretending every village is a separate language.

The Romanian you hear on the news, in school, and in a Bucharest shop is a standard. It was built, from the nineteenth century on, mostly on the speech of Wallachia, the southern dialect often called Muntenian, with choices from the written tradition and from the need to have one language for a new state. A standard is a decision. It is not the only way the language is spoken, and it is not more “correct” in a village than the speech people learned at home. It is the version with a dictionary, a spelling, and a microphone.

Inside Romania, linguists usually group the older regional speech into a handful of dialects rather than a hundred. Muntenian, in the south, is the base of the standard. Moldavian, in the east and in the old Moldavia, is the one other Romanians notice fastest: some vowels sit differently, a few consonants soften, and the melody of a sentence can mark a speaker before any rare word appears. Banat, in the southwest, kept sounds and words from a longer contact with Serbian, German, and Hungarian. Crișana, along the northwest, and Maramureș, in the wooden-church country, have their own bundles of sounds and vocabulary. Transylvanian speech is not one flat dialect. The mountains and the old Saxon and Hungarian towns broke it into smaller habits. A person from a village above Cluj and a person from a Banat town are both “from Transylvania” on a form, and they do not speak the same way.

What changes is often small and systematic. Unstressed vowels shift. The word for yes, or for a tool, or for a cousin, may be the local one. Speed and tone do as much work as vocabulary. None of this stops people understanding each other. A Moldavian and a Muntenian may tease the difference and then finish the meal. The danger in writing about dialects is to turn that tease into a ranking, or into a claim that one region speaks “pure” Romanian. Purity is a myth. Every one of these speeches has borrowed, and the standard has borrowed most visibly, because it had to name trains, offices, and laws.

Across the borders, the same language continues under other conditions. In the Republic of Moldova, the everyday speech is the Moldavian dialect, and the standard used in school has been named both Moldovan and Romanian, according to the politics of the decade. The language is the same language. The argument has been about the name, the alphabet in the Soviet years, and the state, not about whether a sentence from Chișinău is a foreign tongue. North of the Danube in Ukraine, older Romanian-speaking communities remain in Bucovina and the Odessa region. In Serbia, the Romanian-speaking villages of the Banat and the Timoc have their own school and census fights. These are not museum pieces. They are what the old principalities left outside the later border.

South of the Danube, and scattered through the Balkans, are related Eastern Romance languages that are not dialects of the Romanian standard, even when a proud sentence calls them so. Aromanian, spoken in communities in Greece, Albania, North Macedonia, and in towns in Romania after migrations, is the largest. Megleno-Romanian is smaller. Istro-Romanian, on the Istrian peninsula, is very small and close to disappearing as a daily language. They share the Latin skeleton and a long separation. Treating them as “just accents” erases that separation. Treating them as wholly unrelated erases the family. The careful sentence is: one branch of Eastern Romance became the language of the Romanian state. Other branches kept going elsewhere, with fewer speakers and less protection.

If you are learning the language for this site, learn the standard. You will be understood in Constanța and in a Moldavian kitchen. Then listen for the version you meet. A soft consonant, a different word for an uncle, a tune on the end of a question: that is the dialect, still doing its work under the flag and the spelling book.
